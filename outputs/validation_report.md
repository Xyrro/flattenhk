# Validation report

Seven checks of the Hong Kong model against ground truth it was not built from: official bench marks, an independent terrain model, documented gradients, streets known to be level, a trip everyone walks the flat way round, the gaps, and the model's own internal identities. The first four follow upstream's (flattensf's) checks for San Francisco, against Hong Kong's references; every figure below is generated by `python -m hk_flat_routes validate`, and heights are in metres above the Hong Kong Principal Datum.

This run covers **260,082 street edges**, 12,974 km, in 21 pieces with no way between them on foot (landmasses, and whatever the edge of the run cuts off). References outside it are listed as *not in this run*.

## 1. Street elevations against the official bench marks

The Survey and Mapping Office publishes **1,862** bench marks, each a levelled height above the Principal Datum with a position on the HK1980 Grid to the nearest metre. A mark is compared when it stands within 12 m of the centreline of a road that is on the ground; the model's elevation is read from the edge's profile at the nearest point of the centreline.

- Marks compared in this run: **1,325**
- Model minus mark: median **-0.42 m**, median absolute deviation **0.26 m**
- Within 0.5 m: **54%**; within 1 m: **88%**; within 2 m: **97%**

The model is expected to read a few decimetres low. A bench mark's height is that of the top of the mark, which stands about 0.2 m above the ground it is set in, and it is set in the pavement or a wall beside the road, a kerb's height above the carriageway the centreline follows.

The spread is given as a median and a median absolute deviation, not a mean and an RMS, on purpose. A mark's position is good to a metre and it can be up to 12 m to one side of the centreline; on a road cut into a hillside that puts some marks on top of the retaining wall above the road or at the foot of the one below it. Those few differ by metres for reasons that have nothing to do with the terrain model, and an RMS would report little else.

## 2. LiDAR against the independent 5 m terrain model

The Lands Department's 5 m model comes from 2015 aerial photography, shares nothing with the 2020 LiDAR survey, and is on the same grid and datum. Its publisher gives its accuracy as +/- 5 m and says it includes elevated roads and shows the height of the vegetation where land is vegetated; its heights are whole metres. So it is compared only at points on the run's streets (3,997 of them, drawn at random from 80 LiDAR tiles), and the built-up grid, where there is nothing over the street to photograph, is kept apart from the hill roads under trees.

| Street points | Count | Median (LiDAR - 5 m) | MAD | Within 2 m | Within 5 m |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Built-up | 1,903 | -0.90 m | 0.49 m | 84.0% | 92.1% |
| Elsewhere | 2,094 | -0.95 m | 0.59 m | 78.2% | 88.4% |
| All | 3,997 | -0.92 m | 0.54 m | 81.0% | 90.2% |

A built-up point counts as agreeing when the two models are within the 5 m model's own stated accuracy. The LiDAR reads lower, as it should: one model is the ground and the other includes whatever stood on it.

**Verdict:** two independently produced models agree on the street grid to within the coarser one's accuracy, so the LiDAR tiles are correctly georeferenced and in the expected datum throughout. The 5 m model is no use beyond that: section 1 is the measure of accuracy.

## 3. Steep streets against documented gradients

A Hong Kong street name covers kilometres of hairpins, slip roads and flyovers, and the references are sections of it, so each is followed through the network from one documented end to the other and measured on the joined-up elevation profile. *Steepest 50 m* and *steepest 100 m* are the greatest gradients sustained over that length.

## 6. The gaps

The pass analysis finds, exactly, the lowest high point anyone must reach between two places. Between two places either side of one of these gaps the answer on the whole network is rarely the gap, because a walker in no hurry goes round by the coast. So each gap is asked about as a gap is defined: take an official bench mark on the road down each side and only the streets and paths within a circle that just holds both; the lowest crossing between the two marks inside that circle is the model's pass. Beside it is the high point of the shortest walking route between the same marks, found by the router on the whole network. Published heights are OpenStreetMap's for the saddle. They are map readings, and at the gaps where a bench mark stands as well the two differ by several metres, so where a mark stands within 100 m of the mapped saddle its levelled height is given too, and the model is judged against whichever is nearer, to 5 m. A circle can also hold a second, lower gap, and the lowest crossing is then over that one. So when the crossing found is more than 250 m from the mapped saddle it is not taken as this gap's height: the gap is read from the shortest route instead, if that route tops out at the gap, and the verdict says so. The figure judged is the one in bold.

## 8. Targets the model does *not* reproduce

- **Smithfield** (Pok Fu Lam Road to Forbes Street) could not be matched to the network: only 18% of the path between its ends is on the street.
- **Castle Road** (Seymour Road to Robinson Road) is under-reported: 14.3% against the published ratio of 20.0%.
- **Hiram's Highway** (bench marks 220083 to 220084) is under-reported: 7.8% against the gradient between its bench marks of 10.4%.
- **Quarry Gap** comes out 5.7 m higher than its nearer reference: 325.7 m on Mount Parker Road against a mapped 320 m.

None of these was tuned away. What lies behind each kind:

- **Not matched.** The network holds only ways open to walkers or cyclists, under the names the map gives them. A documented section that runs on a flyover, or through a stretch the map tags as a road tunnel, closed to both, or on a road the access rules have (rightly or wrongly) closed, has nothing to be measured on. That is a statement about the street network, not about the elevations.
- **Under-reported.** A published ratio is the steepest pitch, sometimes a few tens of metres long, and on a ladder street that pitch is a flight of steps. The elevation profile is smoothed over about 50 m along the street to suppress survey noise, which takes the top off the shortest pitches. Since the object is to find flat routes, shaving the peak of a wall is a far cheaper error than inventing gradients on level ground.
- **Between bench marks.** Some marks are older than the road now beside them. Where a road has been rebuilt on a new line or level, the nearest centreline to a mark is no longer the road it was levelled on, and the rise between two marks is not the rise of today's road; section 1's largest differences show which marks those are.
- **Gaps.** The published heights are map readings. Where the raw LiDAR at the pass reads as the model does, the difference is in the published figure; where it does not, the model's handling of that edge (a bridge, a flight of steps) has moved it.
